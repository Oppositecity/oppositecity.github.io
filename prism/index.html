PRISM

Paste any text. Every word is scored on thirty-one measured lenses — averages of what thousands of people said a word feels like, looks like, smells like, weighs — and the text is set against English as it is actually used.

The numbers. Each lens is a dataset's own rating scale set to 0–100. On two-sided lenses (essence, pleasure, arousal, control) 50 is neutral; on the rest 0 is none. “In use” weights every rated word by how often it occurs in film and television subtitles (SUBTLEX-US), so common words count more — the fair comparison for running text. “In dict” counts every rated word once. A text's figure averages its rated words, each time they occur. Difference is in points and ratio is text ÷ in use, both against everyday English.

Gaps. Datasets leave words out. The feeling lexicon skips negators and function words like no, not, the; the essence lenses skip yes as well. Unrated words show in italic. Word endings are trimmed simply, so a few forms are missed.

Sources. Essence: the ousiometer of Dodds and colleagues (aggression is their energy axis, the power-plus-danger diagonal). Feeling: NRC VAD Lexicon v2 and labMT happiness. Body: Lancaster Sensorimotor Norms. Substance: Brysbaert concreteness and the Glasgow Norms. Life: Kuperman age of acquisition. Moral: the extended Moral Foundations Dictionary.
